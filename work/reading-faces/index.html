Research study · 2025

Reading Faces

A labelling study on how confidently people tag emotion in faces, and what their uncertainty means for the data AI learns from.

Course
Human Centered AI
Instructor name
Method
Annotation study with confidence ratings
Participants
N classmates
Research question
When people label emotion for AI training data, how much do they agree, and how sure are they?

Emotion recognition systems learn from labels that people assign to faces. Those labels are usually treated as ground truth, but each one is a human judgement. This study looks at what happens inside that judgement.

Method

Participants saw 16 grayscale, front facing portraits from dataset name. Every face shows a neutral expression, so there is no clear right answer. They were given one instruction:

Tag all images of people who are happy

Move the coloured dots over the images based on your perception of which images belong to this category.

High confidenceMediumLow

Each dot records both a label (this face is happy) and how confident the person was. Participants worked on a shared board, so they could see each other’s dots as they placed their own. Confirm whether the board was shared or each person worked alone.

Findings

Three patterns stand out.

  • Nothing was left unlabelled. Asked to find happy people among neutral faces, participants still found them. The task itself nudged people toward an answer.
  • Uncertainty was the norm. Around half of all tags were low confidence, and most faces received only low or medium confidence tags.
  • Agreement was rare. Only faces 3 and 8 drew clusters of high confidence tags. Elsewhere, the same face could be tagged with high confidence by one person and low by another.
Why it matters

In most datasets, a face is tagged happy or not, often by majority vote. The disagreement and low confidence seen here would disappear from the final label. A model trained on that data learns a certainty that the people labelling it never had.

This matters most when emotion inference is used to make decisions about people. My project SafeVision imagined AR glasses that estimate the anxiety of nearby strangers. This study shows why that feature would need far stronger evidence, and why I moved the design away from judging individuals.

Limitations
  • Small sample. N participants from one class.
  • One prompt. Only “happy” was tested, so the results may not hold for other emotions.
  • Hard to trace. Dots were not linked to individual participants, so per person patterns can’t be analysed.
  • Visible answers. On a shared board, early dots may have influenced later ones.
Next steps
  • More prompts, including angry, anxious and trustworthy, to see whether uncertainty varies by emotion.
  • Individual, recorded responses so agreement can be measured properly between participants.
  • Check for bias by comparing tags across the perceived gender and race of the faces.
  • Compare with a model, running the same faces through an emotion recognition system to see where it agrees with people and where it is more confident than they were.
