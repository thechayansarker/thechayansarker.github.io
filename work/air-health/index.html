Where it started

Air Health began as Align, a research project I led from June to September 2025 with Dr. Arnold Mages. Align focused on people recovering from an ACL injury. Recovery takes 9 to 12 months, and most of it happens at home, alone, with no way to know whether you’re doing it right.

81%of people return to some sport after ACL reconstruction.
65%return to the level of sport they played before the injury.
55%return to competitive sport [1].

What I thought I was building

People finish physical therapy, go home with a sheet of exercises, and stop. The fix looked obvious: a better tracker, with clearer instructions and smarter reminders. The research showed I was wrong.

People didn’t stop because they forgot. They stopped because they couldn’t tell whether they were improving, whether their form was right, or how daily habits like sleep and food affected their recovery. As one physiotherapist put it:

“Most patients give up after a week. It’s not that they don’t care, it’s just hard to stay consistent without clear feedback.”

That reframed Align around one question: how might we help ACL patients stay consistent at home by making their recovery progress easier to see?

From research project to startup

Across two rounds of user testing, people showed the strongest interest in real time movement guidance. Air Health, a health tech startup, partnered to build it commercially, and I joined as Senior Product Designer. That shifted the work toward the hardest part of the problem: coaching form through the camera.

Research question
How do you tell someone they’re doing it wrong, in the middle of a rep, in a way they can notice, trust and act on?

Air Health helps people exercise at home with a phone camera that can see how they move and respond in real time. The design challenge sits between the model and the person: a correct prediction is useless if it arrives at the wrong moment, in the wrong form, or feels like criticism.

Related work

HCI research has explored guiding movement with an augmented reality mirror [2], visual guidance and feedback for physiotherapy exercises at home [3], and modelling correct movement from demonstrations so that mistakes can be flagged [4]. Reviews of motor learning show that the channel and timing of feedback matter, and that constant feedback during a movement can leave learners dependent on it [5]. Most of this work assumes a large screen or dedicated sensors in front of the user. Air Health asks what changes when the only sensor is a phone lying on the floor.

My contribution

  • Research: led Align’s research end to end, from patient observation and physiotherapist interviews to synthesis and two rounds of testing, then usability testing for Air Health.
  • Translating model output into feedback: the setup check, the feedback states and cues, and when corrections are spoken, working with the machine learning engineer on which detected errors reach the user.
  • Prototyping: built the interactive prototype myself in SwiftUI and tested it on device.
  • Product design: the adaptive daily plan, session summary and pain check in, and the design system.
Understanding the problem

Methods

The goal was to understand how people navigate long term recovery, and what challenges they face beyond clinical support. Interview sessions ran 30 to 45 minutes.

  • Observed sessions: 4 ACL patients walked me through their recovery exercises while I watched and asked questions.
  • Expert interviews: 2 physiotherapists, on why recovery breaks down once structured therapy ends.
  • Online communities: ACL recovery groups on Reddit, for honest accounts of frustration, fear and small wins.
  • App audits: recovery and wellness apps (Kaia Health, Hinge Health, Calm, Headspace) for Align, then five leading fitness apps for Air Health.
  • User testing: two rounds on Align, then moderated usability sessions of the Air Health prototype with 5 participants.

These were formative studies, meant to guide design decisions rather than to produce findings that generalise. The samples are small, and I treat the patterns below as directions to test further.

What existing apps miss

I audited five leading apps: Ladder, Nike Training Club, Gymshark, MyFitnessPal and Peloton. They are good at workout libraries and tracking history. None of them offered live feedback on form, and none adapted the plan to how the person felt that day. In short, they tell you what you did, not what today needs.

What people told us

I mapped everything into an affinity map, organising patterns across people’s physical, emotional and behavioural recovery. Three themes stood out: motivation fades once progress feels slow; tracking is scattered across separate apps for exercise, sleep and food; and people face their hardest moments alone, because apps can measure pain but can’t understand it.

I synthesised these into three archetypes, the Determined Tracker, the Emotional Healer and the Guided Learner, to keep the design grounded in how different people approach recovery. Later interviews for Air Health added a sharper pattern: people push hard on a day they shouldn’t, feel worse, and then stop going entirely.

Three problems

  1. Adaptation“Same plan, no matter how I feel.” Plans ignore soreness, pain and energy.
  2. Feedback“I don’t know if I’m doing it right.” Without a coach, form errors go unnoticed.
  3. Adherence“Miss a day, quit the plan.” One missed session often ends the routine. Low adherence to prescribed home exercise has been documented for decades [6].

Objective: a clear and trustworthy daily plan that adapts to how your body feels, and coaches you while you move.

How the camera sees

The core loop is simple: the camera captures movement, the system evaluates form, and the plan adapts. Underneath, it runs as a six stage pipeline built with the engineering team.

  1. Frame the bodyBefore a set starts, the app checks that the phone is placed on the floor and the person’s full body is in view. Pose estimation fails quietly when limbs are cut off, so this step protects everything after it.
  2. Estimate the poseGoogle’s MediaPipe Pose Landmarker estimates 33 body landmarks in 3D on every frame, running on the phone [7, 8].
  3. Evaluate formJoint angles derived from the landmarks are compared with a target range for each exercise, so the system can tell when a knee, hip or back drifts from the intended position.
  4. Count repsThe system follows the phases of each movement to count repetitions and time holds.
  5. Coach in the momentForm results are turned into feedback: a skeleton overlay, directional cues and spoken corrections.
  6. Adapt the planSession results and a short pain check in feed tomorrow’s plan, so the app can suggest an easier day after a hard one.
Designing feedback mid rep

I started with storyboards of one person’s journey: setting up a plan around an injury, following guided exercises with live corrections, logging pain, and seeing progress.

From there, most of the design work went into one moment: the correction. I explored three ways to deliver it, each tested on a real phone with the real camera.

1. Skeleton on the body

A skeleton drawn over the live camera view: white while tracking, yellow when a limb drifts from the target, and a pulse when a rep counts. It made the model’s view visible, but reading colour changes on a small screen mid movement is hard.

2. Skeleton and a directional cue

Chevrons were added to show which way to move, with the skeleton turning green when form is correct. This told people what to do, not just that something was wrong. It is shown in motion at the top of this page, along with a short spoken prompt.

3. Demo above, you below

The final direction splits the screen: a demonstration above and the person below, so they can watch the shape and match it. Spoken audio carries the correction, so people don’t have to read the screen at all. The skeleton overlay became optional, with controls for audio, posture overlay, pose estimation and the demo video.

Getting set up

Because everything depends on a clear view of the body, setup became its own flow: choose an exercise, place the phone on the floor, step back until the full body is in view, then a clear confirmation before the set begins.

The adaptive plan

I designed the home screen around one assumption: people open the app asking “what should I do today?”, not “what’s available?” So it leads with the person’s state, then a plan, then one action.

“You seem rested,” based on recent sessions and check ins. A later version sums this up as a daily readiness score.

Closing the loop

After each session, a summary shows the muscles worked, reps completed and motion insights, such as room to squat lower with a wider stance. A quick pain check in on a 0 to 10 scale lets the plan respond to how the body actually feels.

Prototyping and testing

Feedback timing couldn’t be faked in a clickable prototype. A correction that arrives half a second late feels wrong, and only a real camera, real motion and a real phone in hand reveal that. So I built the prototype in SwiftUI and tested it on device, which cut the time from design to a tested interaction by 60%.

I ran moderated usability sessions with 5 participants, each 45 to 60 minutes, with informed consent for recording. Each session followed the full journey: onboarding, positioning the camera, feedback during movement, feedback after the exercise, and the community feed. I mapped each participant’s emotional journey across these phases to find where confidence dropped.

One constraint shaped the final design more than any other. A phone on the floor sits a few feet away and below eye level, so reading it in the middle of a rep means breaking form to look. That is why audio came to carry the correction, and the visual overlay became optional. Testing gave an early signal. The 3 middle aged participants responded well to the visual feedback, but the 2 older participants found the screen very hard to see from that distance. With only five people this is an observation, not a conclusion, but it suggests screen based feedback may not work for older users, and it is something I want to test with a larger and more varied group.

Limitations and next steps

Where the camera can be wrong

  • Viewing conditions. Pose estimation gets less reliable with poor lighting, loose clothing, unusual camera angles and limbs that block each other.
  • Different bodies and eyesight. Target ranges for form need to work across body sizes, ages and mobility levels, and feedback must work for people who can’t see a small screen from the floor, as testing with older participants showed.
  • A camera at home. Recording people in their own space is sensitive. Running pose estimation on the phone keeps video local, but people still need clear control over when the camera is on.
  • Self reported pain is subjective, so the adaptation logic needs careful validation before it can guide rehabilitation.

Next steps

  • Validate the form feedback by comparing the system’s corrections with a physical therapist’s judgement.
  • Compare feedback channels (visual, audio and haptic) to see which ones people notice and act on mid movement.
  • Measure adherence over time, to test whether adaptive plans keep people exercising longer than fixed ones.
What I learned

The first lesson came early: I set out to build a tracker, and the research showed the real problem was feedback and visibility. The second came later, with the camera. The accuracy of the model was never the whole story. The hardest problems were human: when to interrupt, how to phrase a correction so it helps rather than discourages, and how much of the model’s view to show. Moving the correction from the screen to the voice was a small interface change that reflected a big shift in thinking, from showing what the system sees to supporting what the person is doing.

That is the question I want to keep exploring: how can computer vision systems coach people in the moment, in ways that build confidence rather than dependence?
