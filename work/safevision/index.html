Experience design research · 2024

SafeVision

Designing a calm AR experience that helps transit riders feel safer, and working out what such a system should never do.

Role
Experience design & research
Timeline
October 2024 to present
Advisor
Tad Hirsch

Problem

58% of surveyed MBTA riders feel unsafe at night, with few tools to act when something feels wrong.

Approach

Survey, interviews and journey mapping, turned into design principles and a three state AR experience.

Outcome

A speculative concept, a critique of its riskiest assumptions, and a plan to test it with riders.

Research question
How might an AR experience support transit riders’ sense of safety without turning the people around them into suspects?

Riders on Boston’s MBTA describe feeling exposed at night: poorly lit platforms, few visible staff, and little they can do when a situation feels wrong. Existing apps help with schedules and routes, but not with how safe a journey feels.

SafeVision is a speculative concept for AR glasses. It asks how a safety experience could feel calm, give riders control, and stay out of the way. It is experience design, not a built computer vision system: the sensing it imagines is a design assumption, and examining that assumption became a central part of the work.

Understanding riders

I combined four methods to understand when riders feel unsafe, and what they already do about it.

Territory map

Secondary research into the MBTA, mapped as rider groups (commuters, tourists, elderly passengers, people with disabilities) and the issues around them, such as poor signage, missing live information and safety concerns.

Survey

50 respondents recruited on Reddit, asked how safe they feel on the MBTA, especially at night, and which tools they rely on.

58%feel unsafe at night, citing poor lighting, few visible staff and general safety concerns.
23%feel safe, usually on busier routes they know well.
19%say live tracking apps like Transit make them feel more secure.

Interviews

Six semi structured interviews with more than 25 questions, covering travel patterns, tools and moments of feeling unsafe.

“When I’m on the train late at night, I keep checking my surroundings. I just wish I knew who’s around me, it would make me feel calmer.”Participant 4
“If something feels off, I don’t always know what to do. I want to feel like I have some control before things get serious.”Participant 2
“I don’t want the system to make me afraid of people. I want it to help me stay calm and safe, like it’s watching out for me quietly.”Participant 3

Journey map

A real trip from Hung Ave to Park Street. Anxiety peaked while waiting for a delayed train, when a stranger sat close by, and when searching for the right exit in a crowded station.

Limits of this research. The survey is a convenience sample from one online community, and six interviews point to patterns rather than prove them. I treat these findings as direction for design, not as measurement.

Key insights
  1. Unsafe feelings cluster in waiting moments. Delays, empty platforms and late night stops mattered more than time on the train itself.
  2. Riders want awareness, not alarm. People wished they understood their surroundings, but did not want a system that makes them fear other passengers.
  3. Control matters before a crisis. Riders felt most helpless when they sensed something was off but had no clear, low effort way to act.
  4. Existing tools stop at logistics. Transit and Google Maps answer when and where, but not how safe a journey will feel.
Design principles

The insights point to a tension between awareness and fear. I turned them into four principles that every design decision was tested against.

Calm over alarm

The default state should reassure. Alerts are rare, and the interface stays quiet when nothing is wrong.

Control before escalation

Give riders clear options early, before a situation becomes an emergency.

Awareness, not identification

Help riders read the situation without labelling or identifying the people in it.

Heads up, hands free

Keep riders looking at their surroundings, not down at a phone.

The experience

I explored twenty early ideas through sketching, from panic buttons and SOS kiosks to crowd maps and haptic stop alerts. Using the Three Horizons framework, I placed them on a timeline: fixes for today, app features for the near term, and AR glasses as the longer term vision this project focuses on.

The experience is built around three states. The transitions between them matter as much as the states themselves.

Shield

Default while travelling. A quiet green meter signals that nothing needs attention.

→
Danger

Triggered when the situation changes. Offers one tap actions, and the rider decides what happens.

→
Navigate

Guides the rider to safety or their destination, with directions drawn onto the street.

Riders can dismiss Danger at any time to return to Shield, and can start Navigate on demand.

Shield: the calm default

While riding, the glasses show only a quiet green meter, so riders can relax without constantly scanning the carriage.

Danger: escalation with control

If the situation changes, the panel shifts to a warm red meter and offers one tap actions: call emergency services, alert staff, or share location. The rider chooses what happens next, in line with control before escalation.

Navigate: safer routes, heads up

Route guidance is drawn onto the street, with the nearest station and a small map, so riders keep their eyes on their surroundings.

Critique and iteration

In a critique session, participants said feeling safe also depends on understanding how others are feeling. They described moments when someone nearby seemed tense or distracted, and not knowing whether to engage or give space.

This led to an Anxiety Level concept, shown at the top of this page, meant to help riders judge when to talk and when to step back.

It also raised the hardest question in the project, which the next section takes on.

Critical reflection

Looking back, several parts of the concept conflict with its own principles. Naming them is one of the most useful outcomes of the project.

Can feelings be read from faces?

The Anxiety Level feature assumes a system can infer how someone feels from their face. Psychology research questions whether facial movements reliably reveal inner states [1], and face analysis systems have been shown to perform unevenly across skin tones and genders [2]. Inferring a stranger’s emotional state would need far stronger evidence before riders could trust it.

Awareness, not identification

Early mockups show names and occupations for the people around the rider. That breaks the principle of awareness, not identification, and raises questions of bystander consent: the people being analysed never agreed to it.

Whose data, and who gets flagged

The concept assumed sources like public safety records, video feeds and rider reports. Some of these carry historical bias, so the system could flag certain people more often than others, and the groups already over policed on transit would bear the cost of false alarms.

What I would change

  • Remove identity from every state of the interface.
  • Drop inference about individuals and focus on environmental signals like lighting, crowd density and staff presence.
  • Restrict data sources to ones with clear consent and known limitations.
Next steps

The concept hasn’t been tested with riders. The next step is a study that can show whether the experience actually helps.

  • Wizard of Oz study: simulate the three states in a staged station setting, with a researcher triggering transitions, and measure perceived safety and anxiety before and after.
  • Bystander interviews: ask riders how they would feel being seen through someone else’s glasses.
  • A broader sample: recruit beyond one online community, including riders with disabilities and frequent night travellers.
What I learned

Safety is not only about protection. It is also about emotional awareness and social comfort: people want to understand the situation around them, not to be told whom to fear. The hardest design decisions in this project were about what the system should refuse to do.

That is the question I want to keep pursuing: how can AI powered experiences support people’s sense of safety without shifting the risk onto others?
